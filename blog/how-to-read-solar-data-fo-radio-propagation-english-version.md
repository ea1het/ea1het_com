<p>When you look at a solar-terrestrial report, you’ll likely come across various key values like the A-Index and K-Index, among others. These numbers help us understand whether the Earth’s magnetic field is calm or being affected by solar storms.</p>

<h3>Quick summary for people in a hurry  🙂</h3>

<ul>
<li>If you see <strong>high values in SFI, SN, and 304A</strong>, and <strong>low values in A, K, Bz, and Solar Wind</strong>, it’s a <strong>great day for operating on HF</strong>!</li>

<li>On the other hand, if there are <strong>solar storms, high solar wind speeds, or M/X-class flares</strong>, propagation may become difficult.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Full Interpretation of each solar report value</h2>

<h3><strong>K-Index</strong></h3>

<p>Measures rapid changes in Earth’s magnetic field</p>

<ul>
<li>Range: 0 to 9</li>

<li><strong>Low K (0–2):</strong> Very quiet geomagnetic field. Best time for HF operation, especially on high bands like 15 m, 12 m, and 10 m. Signals are stable and conditions are ideal.</li>

<li><strong>Moderate K (3–4):</strong> Slight disturbances, but still good propagation. Some noise or fading may occur.</li>

<li><strong>High K (5+):</strong> Geomagnetic storm. May cause temporary blackouts, lots of noise, and erratic propagation. High bands suffer more, but low bands (80 m, 40 m) may still work fine.</li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>A-Index</strong></h3>

<p>Represents overall magnetic field stability over the past 24 hours</p>

<ul>
<li>Scale: 0 to 400</li>

<li><strong>Low A (0–7):</strong> Stable magnetic field, usually aligned with excellent propagation.</li>

<li><strong>Medium A (8–15):</strong> Active but still usable conditions. A good time to observe how bands behave.</li>

<li><strong>High A (16+):</strong> Indicates major storms or unstable conditions. Propagation becomes unpredictable.</li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>SFI (Solar Flux Index)</strong></h3>

<p>Measures solar radiation at a specific frequency</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Above 100:</strong> Good conditions on high bands (20 m, 17 m, 15 m, 12 m, 10 m)</li>

<li><strong>Below 70:</strong> High bands likely closed or weak<br><strong>→ Higher is better for DX on high bands</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>SN (Sunspot Number)</strong></h3>

<p>Visible sunspot count. Sunspots help ionize the atmosphere, improving propagation</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Above 50:</strong> Very good signal for HF propagation</li>

<li><strong>Low or 0:</strong> Likely poor propagation<br><strong>→ More sunspots = better conditions</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>X-Ray (Solar Flares)</strong></h3>

<p>Indicates solar flare strength</p>

<ul>
<li>Class levels: A, B, C, M, X (from weakest to strongest)</li>

<li><strong>C-Class:</strong> Moderate flare</li>

<li><strong>M or X-Class:</strong> Can cause HF blackouts on Earth’s sunlit side<br><strong>→ Watch out for M or X: risk of temporary HF shutdowns</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>304A (304 Angstrom Flux)</strong></h3>

<p>Measures extreme ultraviolet radiation affecting the ionosphere</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Above 100:</strong> Good ionization, great for long-distance (DX) propagation<br><strong>→ High value = excellent conditions for skip propagation</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>Proton Flux (Ptn Flx)</strong></h3>

<p>Number of protons arriving from the sun</p>

<ul>
<li>High levels signal solar storms that can interfere with HF and damage satellites<br><strong>→ If high, propagation may become erratic</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>Electron Flux (Elc Flx)</strong></h3>

<p>Measures energetic electrons in the magnetosphere</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Moderate-high:</strong> Helps high-band propagation</li>

<li><strong>Too high:</strong> Can cause noise or signal absorption<br><strong>→ Good for propagation if it doesn’t spike too much</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>Aurora</strong></h3>

<p>Measures auroral activity</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Below 5:</strong> Normal conditions</li>

<li><strong>Above 5:</strong> Active auroras can negatively affect HF, especially at high latitudes<br><strong>→ More aurora = worse HF propagation</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>Bz (Interplanetary Magnetic Field Component)</strong></h3>

<p>Shows how the solar magnetic field is interacting with Earth’s</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Negative (–):</strong> Can allow solar particles in, triggering auroras or disruptions</li>

<li><strong>Near 0 or positive (+):</strong> Calm conditions<br><strong>→ Strongly negative Bz = potential geomagnetic storm</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>Solar Wind</strong></h3>

<p>Measures the speed of solar wind impacting Earth</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Below 400 km/s:</strong> Calm</li>

<li><strong>400–600 km/s:</strong> Normal but a bit unsettled</li>

<li><strong>Above 600 km/s:</strong> Potential geomagnetic storm<br><strong>→ Faster wind = more chance of instability</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3><strong>GeoMag Field</strong></h3>

<p><strong>Overall state of Earth’s magnetic field</strong><br>Can be labeled as: <code>Quiet</code>, <code>Unsettled</code>, <code>Active</code>, <code>Storm</code>, etc.<br><strong>→ Quiet = ideal radio conditions</strong></p>

<h3><strong>Sig Noise Level (Atmospheric Noise)</strong></h3>

<p>Baseline noise in S-units visible on your receiver</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>S1–S2:</strong> Almost no noise, perfect conditions</li>

<li><strong>S5 or more:</strong> Lots of noise, harder to hear weak signals<br><strong>→ Lower is better for reception</strong></li>
</ul>
