<p>Radio propagation is the behavior of radio waves as they travel from one point to another. As a form of electromagnetic radiation, radio waves are affected by reflection, refraction, diffraction, absorption, polarization, and scattering.</p>

<figure><img loading="lazy" decoding="async" width="1024" height="718" src="/assets/images/20260215-001.png" alt="" /></figure>

<h2>Quick resume of the following complete table:</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Low HF (160m–80m):</strong> Controlled mainly by D-layer absorption and nighttime F-layer reflection.</li>

<li><strong>Mid HF (40m–20m):</strong> Dominated by F2 propagation and grey line effects.</li>

<li><strong>High HF (15m–10m):</strong> Strongly solar-cycle dependent; Sporadic E plays a major role.</li>

<li><strong>VHF/UHF (6m–70cm):</strong> Mostly line-of-sight, but enhanced by Es, ducting, aurora, meteor scatter, and TEP.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Why This Matters</h2>

<ul>
<li>When you hear a signal from 2000 km away on 6 meters in June, it’s probably <strong>Sporadic E</strong>.</li>

<li>When 20 meters stays open all night during solar peak, that’s <strong>strong F2 propagation</strong>.</li>

<li>When 2 meters suddenly carries signals 1500 km across the sea, that’s likely <strong>tropospheric ducting</strong>.</li>

<li>Recognizing these patterns urns random luck into informed operating strategy — and that’s where radio becomes truly fascinating.</li>
</ul>
